Write and typeset a document — something that would survive being printed and read at a desk. Not a web page, not a dashboard, not a landing page with a hero.

The paper. A stack of A4 sheets, each exactly 794px wide by 1123px tall, one under the next with 24px between them, centred on a plain ground with a soft shadow. Each sheet has a 72px margin on every side; at its head a running header under a hairline — the document's title on the left, what it is for on the right, both small and in the sans — and at its foot a running footer above a hairline: who it is for on the left, the month and year on the right. Both repeat on every sheet, which is what makes it printable as-is. Break the content into as many sheets as it needs — a document that says "4 to 6 pages" means four to six of these — and never let a block cross a sheet's edge. Set the page in the markup: do not rely on print CSS the reader will never trigger.

The type. A serif for everything read — the body, the headings, the lists — and a sans or mono kept for labels: table column heads, side-headings, the running header and footer, a caption, the small caps over a box. Unless a design system says otherwise use Newsreader for the body and IBM Plex Sans for the labels, loaded from Google Fonts with an @import and each given a real fallback stack. Body at 16px with a line height near 1.55, and a measure of about 640px, which is roughly 75 characters. Nothing that is read at length goes below 15px.

The hierarchy, and nothing else in it: - The title, once, on the first sheet: large in the serif, with a one-line standfirst under it in the muted ink saying what the document is and who it is for, over a hairline. - Sections numbered from 1, set as the number, a wide gap, then the title — "3  Where value has actually appeared" — about 22px, semibold, in the serif. Leave real space above each one. - Sub-sections only if a section truly needs them, at about 17px, semibold, no number. - Never skip a level, and never use size alone where a heading belongs.

How many columns a table can carry is decided by the measure, not by how many things you want to say. About 650px holds three or four columns of prose; a fifth turns every cell into a seven-line paragraph two words wide, and the table grows until it runs off the page. If a cell wraps past three lines the table has a column too many: merge two, move one into the running text, or split it into two tables. A column carrying sentences is never narrower than 150px.

The devices a document has, used where they earn it and not otherwise: - A table: column heads in the sans, uppercase, letterspaced, about 10px, in the muted ink, over a rule; rows divided by hairlines and nothing between the columns; a numbered caption BELOW it in the sans at 12px muted — "Table 1. Maturity assessed by task class rather than by product." Where a column carries a judgement, colour it on a scale — green for settled, amber for conditional, red for not yet — and let that be the only colour on the page. - A side-heading block: a short label in the sans in the left margin — "Phase 1", "Q3", "Article 4" — with the passage beside it, entries divided by hairlines. This is how a document does a timeline; it does not need cards. - A pull-out band: two columns of short passages, each with a 3px rule down its left edge, a bold lead-in and two or three sentences. A rule, not a box. - A numbered list where the order matters, each entry opening with a bold lead-in phrase and a full stop, then the sentence that explains it.
- Where a section makes several points in turn, open each paragraph with a bold lead-in of three or four words and a full stop — "First, the systems are general." — which is the plainest way to make prose scannable without turning it into a list. - A callout: a short passage set apart on a tinted ground with a hairline border and 24px of padding, opened by a small uppercase letterspaced label in the sans — THE USEFUL TEST, WHAT THIS MEANS, A WORKED EXAMPLE. Two or three sentences, and the claim it turns on may be italic.
- An action block, where the document asks for something: the same tinted ground but with a 3px rule down its left edge in the accent, a label such as DECISIONS REQUESTED or WHAT WE NEED FROM YOU, and a numbered list, each entry one sentence naming who does what.
- A blockquote or an epigraph, indented, in the serif, without quotation marks.

What it must not become: a card grid, a set of metric tiles, a sidebar, a hero, a call-to-action button, a pill, a progress bar, an emoji, or a screen of any kind. There is no navigation in a document. Colour appears in exactly two places — a table's judgement column, and the rule down the left edge of an action block — and nowhere else; everything else is ink, muted ink and hairlines on white. A callout is a tinted ground with no colour in its text.

Write it properly. Every section has real prose, in full sentences, saying something specific enough to be wrong — a document that could be about any subject is a template, not a document. Prefer the concrete claim to the general one, name the thing rather than gesture at it, and let the last line of a section land rather than trail off. Do not pad to reach a length; if a section has three paragraphs in it, write three.
