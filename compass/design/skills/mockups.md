Design an operational prototype, not a picture of one: the screens of a real product, in one document, that someone can click through.

The shell, drawn once and never redrawn between screens. Build it to these measurements — a shell that is merely described comes out loose, and a loose shell is the first thing a person sees.
- The page is a two-column grid: 236px of sidebar and the rest, 100vh tall, the sidebar flush to the window on three sides with no margin, no radius and no gap. The sidebar is a flex column on the ink ground; the canvas beside it scrolls on its own.
- At the sidebar’s top, a brand block with 16px of padding: a 36px tile at 10px radius holding two initials in white on deep teal, beside the product name at 15px/600 in white with, under it, a 10px uppercase 0.1em-tracked line naming the department in the idle grey. A hairline closes the block.
- Then the nav, 8px of padding, each item a 40px row at 10px radius with 10px of gap: a 16px stroked icon, then a 13px label. The current item is filled #22272A with white text; the rest are the idle grey and lift to #1A1E21 under the pointer. A section holding a queue carries its count pushed to the right edge as a small filled pill.
- Then a spacer that takes the slack (margin-top:auto), so the signed-in person sits at the very bottom of the sidebar, inside it, above a hairline, on the sidebar’s own ground: a 32px circular avatar with initials, the name at 13px/600 in white, the role at 11px in the idle grey under it, and a chevron pushed to the right edge. It shares the nav’s left padding, so the avatar’s left edge and the nav icons’ left edges are the same line. A profile that floats in the middle of the column, overlaps the last nav item, sits on the canvas instead of the sidebar, or is clipped by the sidebar’s bottom edge is the plainest sign the shell was drawn rather than built.
- Every screen opens the same way, and the opening is identical on all of them: a header row with 32px of padding holding, on the left, an 11px uppercase kicker naming the area over the 28px screen title; and pushed to the right, on the same centre line, the controls — a search field, the scope or period selector, and one primary button in deep teal. Every control in that row is 36px tall and 8px radius.
The screens: one section element per screen, all present in the document, only the current one shown. Clicking a sidebar item shows that screen and marks it current. Every screen the brief names gets built — six to nine of them — with an overview first, and the sidebar carries a count beside any section holding a queue.
Every control works. Tabs filter the rows under them and carry counts. A checkbox in a row selects it and raises a bulk bar that counts what is selected and totals what matters. A row's menu opens. A filter changes what is listed. A field takes typing. Nothing is a drawing of a control. An action that would leave the prototype — sending, approving, exporting, inviting — raises a small notice in the corner saying what happened, which fades on its own, and leaves the row or the counter in the state that action would have produced. One notice element, reused, with a line of script to show it.
Where the brief asks for options — two ways to lay a screen out, two models for an interaction — build them as a toggle inside that screen, not as separate designs.
One consistent world of data: the same people, clients and projects on every screen, numbers that agree with each other — a total that matches its rows, a bar that matches its percentage, a badge that matches the queue it counts.
Fill every screen. A screen is a working page, not a header and a gap: a row of four metric tiles, each with its label, a large number, the change on it (+2.1, -1.3) and a line of context; then a band of two, a chart on the left and the queue of things wanting attention on the right, each item with its own number; then the table of that section's records under a strip of tabs that carry counts — eight rows or more, seven or so columns, a progress bar where something is being burned through, a status pill, a quiet row action. Nothing on a screen ends in a band of empty ground; if a screen has run out of things to say, it has not been thought about hard enough.
Charts are drawn, not implied: bars across six or so periods with their values above them, a legend naming each series, labels along the bottom. Inline SVG or CSS, never an image, never a lone sparkline standing in for a chart.
Colour carries meaning, and different meanings take different colours. The shell is ink and the canvas is paper — those never take a hue. Everything countable does: each chart takes the colour its measure has earned rather than the colour the last chart used, a meter is filled by the band its value falls in, a heat cell is tinted by its band under a legend that names the bands, and a status pill and the rail down its row agree. Six panels of data should read as six colours; a screen in one colour is a screen with nothing to say, and a screen whose panels are all tinted is one that has stopped saying anything.
Set every screen in the two faces: the sans for names, labels and headings, the mono for money, hours, percentages, ids, dates and every numeric cell, with tabular figures so a column lines up. The big number on a metric tile is mono at 26px; its label is an 11px uppercase letterspaced sans; its context line is 13px muted sans.
Design the states, not just the full one: what a list looks like with nothing in it yet and what to do about that, what it looks like while it loads — skeleton rows, never a spinner — and what a field looks like when its value is wrong. A form puts its label above its input, in one column, and says what went wrong next to the thing that is wrong.
Before you finish, take a last look: every space on the scale, the hierarchy obvious at a glance, contrast that passes, keyboard focus you can see, empty and loading states drawn, and the whole thing read once with the real content in it.
Tables get tabular figures, numbers to the right, a status pill and a quiet row action. A table that fills a panel is width: 100% with table-layout: fixed and column widths that add up to 100% — left to lay itself out, a ten-column table sizes to its content, runs past the panel's edge, and the panel's overflow quietly cuts the last column off. If the columns will not fit at a readable width, carry one fewer.
Write the content into the markup — every row, every bar, every number, every label. The script only shows and hides, filters, toggles, selects and counts. Delete the script and the prototype must still be a full, readable design; a screen that is empty until JavaScript fills it is one syntax error away from nothing at all.
Wire it with one small script at the end: navigation, tabs, selection, the counters that follow from them. Keep it simple enough to be obviously correct. No frameworks, no libraries.
